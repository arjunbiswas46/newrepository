Growth plan · Siliguri, West Bengal

Cookie Shop Growth Plan

How a cookie business grows from one store to five and beyond while the owner never works in it. Paid staff make the dough, bake and sell from day one. The owner spends about 4 hours a week checking numbers, approving money and choosing people. One kitchen makes all the dough; stores only bake and sell.

Built on the figures in the Cookie Shop Profit Planner (75 g cookie, about ₹23.50 cost, ₹60 average price, 5% GST, 8% waste) and the Operations Playbook. Rents, salaries and sales per store are estimates; replace them with real numbers as you go.

Your 4 hours a week

You own the business; you are not one of its workers. You work on it: you set the rules, read the numbers, approve money and choose the leaders. You never make dough, stand at the counter or deliver.

What only you keep

  1. The bank account. Staff never have bank login or a cheque book. Salaries and supplier bills go out from your phone, against bills approved in the app.
  2. The billing app's owner login. Staff can bill and record stock, but cannot delete bills, change prices or edit recipes.
  3. The recipes. Only the kitchen lead holds them, under a written confidentiality clause in their appointment letter.
  4. Hiring and firing leaders. Leaders hire their own teams, but you choose the leaders.

Setup still needs you. Before opening and for the first 6 to 8 weeks, plan on 15 to 20 hours a week: finding the site, licences, hiring, setting up the billing app and watching the first weeks. That is a project you finish, not a job. If you can't give that time, pay a bakery consultant to set up recipes and train the kitchen lead.

The model

This is a hub-and-spoke business. One central kitchen (the hub) makes, scoops and freezes dough. Every store (a spoke) gets frozen dough each morning and bakes it fresh through the day. Customers get the same cookie at every store, and store staff need only one skill: baking from frozen and selling.

Why this grows well:

  1. Same taste everywhere. One team mixes every batch, so a recipe can't drift from store to store.
  2. Smaller, cheaper stores. Without a mixer, wash area and dough storage, a store needs 100 to 200 sq ft, so a kiosk or a small counter works.
  3. Easier hiring. Salespeople learn to bake from frozen in a day. Only the kitchen needs a skilled baker.
  4. Better buying. One kitchen buys butter and chocolate for every store, so you get bulk prices. The plan assumes cost per cookie falls from ₹23.50 to ₹22 at three stores and ₹21.50 at five.

Start with the kitchen in store 1's back room. Rent a first store of about 300 sq ft with a back room. The kitchen lead mixes dough there and bakes in the same shop, so there is no second rent and no delivery. At 250 cookies a day this makes about ₹1,06,500 a month, against about ₹81,000 with a separate kitchen from day one. The back room can supply up to roughly 1,000 dough balls a day, which covers three stores. Move to a separate central kitchen at store 4 or 5.

  • 2 stores: adds store 2 (₹80,500: rent ₹35,000, power ₹8,000, two salespeople ₹20,000, billing ₹2,500, repairs ₹3,000, marketing ₹5,000, fit-out loan ₹7,000), a kitchen helper ₹11,000, a part-time dough rider ₹5,000, and a ₹7,000 raise for the kitchen lead, who now runs both stores.
  • 3 stores: the kitchen lead becomes operations manager at ₹25,000. A new baker ₹15,000 and helper ₹11,000 run the back-room kitchen. Rider ₹9,000. Store 3 adds another ₹80,500.
  • 5 stores: a separate central kitchen (₹1,19,500 with rent, two bakers, two helpers and a full-time rider), operations manager and a part-time accounts clerk (₹35,000), store 1 without its kitchen costs, and four small stores.

If the dough moved to a separate kitchen from day one, store 1 would make about ₹81,000 a month and need 160 cookies a day to break even. Two salespeople at ₹10,000 each is below the West Bengal minimum wage of ₹10,558 a month for unskilled shop staff in city areas (from July 2026); paying the legal minimum adds about ₹1,100 per store per month, plus Puja bonus.

When to open the next store

Opening too early is the most common way small chains fail: the new store drains cash and the owner's attention before the first one is stable. Open the next store only when every box below is true.

The four stages

At every stage there is one paid person who answers to you. Their title grows with the business; your 4 hours a week don't.

Stage 1
Months 0–9
One store with a back-room kitchen. A kitchen lead makes dough, bakes and runs the shop day to day. Two salespeople sell in two shifts; the senior one is shop lead.
  • Who answers to you: the kitchen lead, paid ₹18,000 plus 10% of profit above break-even.
  • Build: the playbook systems, billing app with recipes and stock, nightly report.
  • Done when: 3 profitable months in a row and every gate above passes.
Stage 2
Months 9–15
Store 2, supplied from store 1's back room. Hire a kitchen helper a month early so the kitchen lead has time to set up store 2. A part-time rider takes frozen dough to store 2 each morning.
  • Who answers to you: the kitchen lead, now running both stores, at ₹25,000 plus bonus.
  • Build: the new-store kit, store-by-store profit report, stock transfer between stores.
  • Done when: store 2 is above its own break-even (about 91 cookies a day) for 3 months.
Stage 3
Year 2
Store 3 and delivery. The kitchen lead becomes operations manager and visits every store each day. A new baker takes over the kitchen. Add Swiggy and Zomato orders, and supply cafés and hotels from spare kitchen capacity.
  • Who answers to you: the operations manager, with a bonus on profit across all stores.
  • Build: bulk buying contracts, a generator, a search for central kitchen space.
Stage 4
Year 3 onward
Central kitchen, stores 4 and 5, then nearby towns. Move dough-making to a separate kitchen when the back room passes about 1,000 dough balls a day. Then Jalpaiguri, and tourist towns such as Darjeeling, Kalimpong and Gangtok, where gift boxes sell well. Frozen dough travels a few hours in insulated boxes; for longer trips, use a refrigerated van or a second kitchen.
  • Who answers to you: still the operations manager; add a part-time accounts clerk.
  • Option: franchising, only after 5 company stores make money and the brand name is a registered trademark. Franchise owners run their own stores, which suits a business with an absent owner.

The central kitchen

Setting it up

  • Space: start in store 1's back room (about 100 sq ft is enough for a mixer, a freezer and a table). When you move out, take 300 to 500 sq ft on a back street, not the high street. Both need water, drainage, a wash area and a strong power supply.
  • Licences: while dough is made in store 1, make sure its FSSAI licence covers manufacturing and supplying your other stores. A separate kitchen needs its own FSSAI licence and trade licence. Each store also needs its own FSSAI licence or registration and trade licence. Confirm with your CA.
  • Equipment, about ₹3 to 4 lakh: a 20-litre planetary mixer, two chest freezers, steel work tables, scales, scoops, food-grade crates, insulated boxes with ice packs, and an inverter or generator.
  • Capacity: a 20-litre mixer handles very roughly 6 to 8 kg of cookie dough a batch, which is 80 to 105 cookies of 75 g. Measure your real output in the first month. Past about 1,000 dough balls a day, add a second shift or a 40-litre mixer.

Two rules that stop most problems

  1. Every dough ball is counted. For each store, dough received must equal cookies sold plus waste plus dough left in the freezer. A gap of more than 1% is checked the same day.
  2. Every store keeps 2 days of dough in its own freezer. A power cut, a sick baker or a strike day at the kitchen then doesn't close any store. Use the oldest dough first, and set a use-by of 30 days from the batch date.

Keep stores 2 to 3 km apart and aim each at a different crowd: shoppers, students, office workers or travellers. Two stores on the same street mostly split the same customers.

The most important hire is the first kitchen lead. Look for an experienced baker who has run a small bakery or café kitchen and wants to grow into a manager. Pay a little above market and add the profit bonus; a good one is cheaper than you working in the shop yourself. Promote from inside after that: a salesperson who knows the routines makes a good shop lead, and a good shop lead can become the next operations manager.

New-store kit

Write this once, after store 1, and reuse it for every store. Opening store 5 should feel like repeating a checklist, not starting over.

  1. Site checklist: footfall count, rent limit (under 15% of expected sales), power load, signboard visibility, distance from your other stores.
  2. Fit-out pack: one layout drawing, one equipment list, the same counter, display and signboard from the same vendors.
  3. Licence list: FSSAI, trade licence and Shops and Establishments for the new address, applied for the day the rent agreement is signed.
  4. Hiring pack: job post, interview questions, paid trial shift. Hire 3 weeks before opening.
  5. Training: the 5-day plan, with days 2 to 5 done at an existing store.
  6. Billing setup: copy store 1's menu, recipes and alerts into the new outlet in the billing app.
  7. Launch: free samples for the first 3 days, Instagram posts, flyers to nearby colleges and offices.

Fund store 2 mostly from store 1's profit, with a Mudra loan to close the gap. Because store 2 gets dough from store 1's back room, it needs no mixer, so its setup cost sits at the low end of the range. Don't take on a second loan while the first new store is still below its break-even. A good rule: each new store should earn back its setup cost within 12 months. If one doesn't, fix it or close it before opening the next.
